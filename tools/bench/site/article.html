The Keepers of Gull Point Light

A long read about one small lighthouse and the families who kept it burning. About 12 minutes.

Gull Point juts out from the northern shore like a crooked finger, and for most of the nineteenth century it was the place where ships came to grief. Fog rolls in from the strait with little warning, the tide runs hard across the shoal, and the rocks below the bluff are invisible at high water. Fishermen in the harbor town told stories of whole crews lost within sight of their own chimneys.

Figure 1. The bluff at Gull Point seen from the harbor, around 1890.

A tower on the bluff

After a particularly bad winter in which three schooners were wrecked, the harbor merchants petitioned for a light. Construction began the following spring. The first tower was built in 1871 from granite quarried a few miles inland and hauled to the bluff by ox cart. It stood forty-two feet tall and carried a fixed white light that could be seen, on a clear night, about fourteen miles out to sea.

The first keeper, Thomas Arden, arrived with his wife Margaret and their four children. The keeper's house had two rooms downstairs, two rooms under the roof, a cistern for rain water and a small barn. Supplies came by boat once a month, weather permitting, which in winter meant rarely.

Figure 2. Plan of the keeper's house, drawn from the original 1871 contract.

Oil, wicks and long nights

Keeping the light was relentless work. Every evening the keeper trimmed the wicks, filled the reservoir with whale oil and later with kerosene, and polished the glass until it shone. Through the night someone climbed the stairs every few hours to check the flame. The logbooks that survive record the weather, passing ships and the occasional rescue in a careful, unhurried hand.

Margaret Arden kept the light alone for eleven weeks in 1879 while her husband recovered from a broken leg. Her entries in the log are shorter than his, and there is not a single missed night.

Figure 3. A page from the 1879 logbook in Margaret Arden's handwriting.

The new lens

In 1902 the original lamp was replaced with a fourth-order Fresnel lens shipped from France in eleven crates. The new lens turned the fixed light into a flashing one, a white flash every six seconds, so that sailors could tell Gull Point from the lights at neighbouring harbors. The keeper at the time, William Arden, the founder's grandson, wrote that the lens was "the most beautiful object ever to come up our road."

Figure 4. The Fresnel lens shortly after installation.

Electric light

Electricity reached the point in 1934, when a line was strung along the coast road to serve the new fish cannery. The light was electrified the same year. Oil was kept on hand for emergencies for another decade, and the keepers continued to live at the station, maintain the equipment and sound the fog bell by hand when the signal failed.

The Second World War brought a small naval lookout post to the bluff. Sailors slept in the barn, and the light was dimmed on orders from the coast command for most of 1942.

Figure 5. The naval lookout post on the bluff, 1943.

The last keepers

After the war the station passed to the Coast Guard. Keepers now served rotating tours rather than whole lifetimes, and few stayed longer than three years. The house was modernised, the barn was torn down, and a radio beacon was added beside the tower.

The station's long era of resident keepers ended quietly. The Coast Guard automated the Gull Point light in 1987, installing a solar-charged beacon and a fog detector, and the last keeper, Daniel Ruiz, locked the door of the house for the final time that autumn.

Figure 6. Daniel Ruiz on his last day at the station.

The museum

For years the empty house was boarded up. A volunteer society formed to save it, raised money with bake sales and lighthouse tours, and opened a small museum in the keeper's house in 2004. The Fresnel lens is on display in the old parlor, and the logbooks can be read by appointment.
